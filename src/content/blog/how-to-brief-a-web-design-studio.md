*By [Geetha](https://www.linkedin.com/in/geethaspecialist), co-founder of Uncoded Hub, where she leads project delivery. Verified as of September 10, 2026.*

Two businesses hire the same studio for a comparable project. One arrives with a vague brief, wanting a professional website, modern, showcasing our services, and spends the first two weeks in back-and-forth establishing basic direction. The other arrives with a clear, specific brief, and the same studio delivers weeks faster, with fewer revision rounds and less friction throughout. The studio didn't change. The brief did.

## The short answer

A brief that speeds up a project answers the questions a studio would otherwise have to ask, one at a time, over multiple rounds. Who the site is for. What it needs to do. What "done" looks like. What already exists to work from. A vague brief risks a worse outcome and directly costs real project time, regardless of how fast the studio works. This is also the single biggest lever on whether a fixed delivery timeline actually holds, since our own seven-day clock only starts once a brief like this is locked.

## Separate what you're selling from what customers are buying

[*Business Development For Dummies*](https://www.goodreads.com/en/book/show/25269367-business-development-for-dummies) makes a point worth applying directly to briefing: separate what customers are buying from what you're selling, since customers often want the underlying outcome rather than the literal deliverable you think you're providing. Before briefing a studio, apply this to your own business first. Are you selling a website, or is the real outcome you need more enquiries, more credibility, or faster quote turnaround? A brief built around the real outcome produces a genuinely different, more useful project than one built around a vague deliverable description.

## Build your own customer avatar before handing off the brief

Ryan Deiss and Russ Henneberry's method from [*Digital Marketing For Dummies*](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/33645930-digital-marketing-for-dummies), build a specific, named customer avatar for each distinct segment you serve since broad campaigns aimed at everyone resonate with no one, is work a client should do before briefing a studio rather than leaving for the studio to guess at. A studio told "we serve small businesses" has to guess at tone, structure and priorities. A studio told exactly who the site needs to convert, and what that person cares about, moves immediately rather than spending early project time extracting this through discovery calls.

## State your organising principle up front

Michael Masterson's advice from [*Ready, Fire, Aim*](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/2490652.Ready_Fire_Aim), build a Unique Selling Proposition and make it the organising principle for all your marketing copy rather than an afterthought, applies directly to a good brief. What makes this business different from its competitors should be stated plainly at the start rather than left for a studio to infer or ask about repeatedly across multiple revision rounds.

## What a genuinely good brief includes

Who the site is for, specifically, rather than a broad audience description. The kind of avatar detail covered above.

What the site needs to do, tied to a real business outcome rather than only "look professional".

What already exists, honestly. Logo, brand colours, existing copy, past marketing material, even if imperfect, since a studio building from nothing takes longer than one adapting existing material.

A real example of what good looks like, whether from a competitor's site or any other reference, even an imperfect one. This aligns expectations faster than an abstract description ever could.

Honest constraints stated upfront, covering actual budget range, actual timeline and any non-negotiable requirements, rather than surfacing these only when they conflict with something already built.

## What slows a project down

A brief vague on purpose, hoping the studio will figure out what works, transfers strategic decisions that should have been made by the business owner onto the studio, and every undecided item becomes a round-trip conversation consuming real project time. A brief changing mid-project, with new priorities emerging that weren't part of the original scope, has the same effect regardless of how clear the original brief was.

## What to prepare before the first conversation

1. Write down the real business outcome you need the site to produce rather than only "a website".
2. Define your specific customer avatar per the method above, rather than a broad audience description.
3. Gather what already exists, covering brand assets, existing copy and past material, even if imperfect.
4. Find one real example of a site whose approach you genuinely like, and be specific about what you like about it.
5. State your actual budget and timeline constraints upfront rather than letting them surface only when they conflict with what's already built.

## The honest limits of this

Even a genuinely excellent brief doesn't guarantee a fast project if the studio's own process is undisciplined. The client-side preparation covered here is necessary rather than sufficient, and it works best paired with a studio having the documented, repeatable process covered in [7-Day Website Design Is It Actually Possible](/blog/seven-day-website-design-is-it-possible).

## Frequently asked questions

**What makes a web design brief actually speed up a project?**
Answering the questions a studio would otherwise ask across multiple rounds, covering who the site is for, what outcome it needs to produce, what already exists to build from, and honest budget and timeline constraints stated upfront.

**Why does a vague brief cost real project time rather than only risking a worse outcome?**
Every undecided item in a vague brief becomes a round-trip conversation during the project, which is time a clear, specific brief would have saved by answering those questions before the project started.

**What's the single most useful thing to prepare before briefing a studio?**
A specific, real understanding of who the site needs to convert and what outcome it needs to produce. Not a generic audience description, but the kind of detail letting a studio move immediately rather than guessing.
