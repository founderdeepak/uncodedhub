*By [Geetha](https://www.linkedin.com/in/geethaspecialist), co-founder of Uncoded Hub, where she leads project delivery. Verified as of September 10, 2026.*

A single headline number, "websites starting from a low figure", tells you almost nothing about what you're buying. Two quotes with the same headline shape can represent wildly different scopes, and the gap only becomes visible once the project is underway and something you assumed was included turns out not to be.

The useful question isn't what the number is. It's what's inside it.

## The short answer

A small business website's cost breaks into a handful of real components: design and build, copywriting, image and media handling, basic SEO groundwork, and ongoing maintenance. Each can be included, excluded or partially included in ways that change the total meaningfully. Comparing two quotes by their headline figure alone, without checking what's inside each, compares two different products as if they were the same one.

## Anchoring on the headline number

Dan Ariely's finding in [*Predictably Irrational*](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/1713426.Predictably_Irrational), we rarely judge value in absolute terms and instead compare against whatever reference point is nearby, is exactly why headline-number comparison is so easy to fall into and so unreliable. The lowest number you've seen becomes the reference point every other quote gets measured against, regardless of whether it represents the same scope. Two quotes differing meaningfully on paper might represent nearly identical scope with different margins, or genuinely different products, and the number alone can't tell you which.

## What actually goes into the number

Design and build. The base cost of structuring and building the pages, which is usually what a headline figure quotes and usually the smallest source of real variation between studios doing comparable work.

Copywriting. Who writes the actual page text, whether you, the studio, or some collaborative process, changes the number substantially. A studio quote excluding copywriting looks cheaper on paper while shifting real hours onto you, which is a cost even when it isn't a line item.

Image and media handling. Correct sizing, compression and optimisation of whatever photography or graphics the site uses. Easy to skip in a cheap quote and expensive to fix later, since a slow, unoptimised site is close to the most common reason a finished small business site underperforms.

Basic on-page SEO. Meta titles and descriptions, a sitemap, basic structured data. The groundwork making the site findable at all. Often sold as a separate add-on rather than included, which is fine as long as it's disclosed rather than silently assumed.

Ongoing maintenance and updates. What happens after launch. Who fixes something that breaks, who updates content, whether hosting and domain renewal are handled or left to you. A near-zero maintenance cost at launch often means this cost arrives later, unplanned.

## Own a category, don't compare across categories

Al Ries' rule from [*The 22 Immutable Laws of Marketing*](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/33449.The_22_Immutable_Laws_of_Marketing), own one word in the prospect's mind since a narrow position beats a broad one, is a useful lens for a mistake business owners make constantly: comparing a template-based DIY builder's price against a custom-built, fixed-price studio quote as if they're the same category. They aren't. One is a tool you operate yourself. The other is a delivered, done-for-you outcome. Comparing their prices directly, without accounting for what each produces, compares categories never meant to be judged by the same number.

## A documented process is what makes a number trustworthy

The core argument of [*The E-Myth Revisited*](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/81948.The_E_myth_Revisited), build your business as a documented, replicable system, applies to how a studio should justify its own number. A studio able to show you exactly what a given price includes, broken into the components above, with a repeatable process behind each, is one whose number you can trust. One giving you a single figure with no breakdown is asking you to trust a black box. We can state one component of that process plainly, without needing to itemise a figure: our delivery clock is fixed at seven days once scope is locked, and it applies the same way regardless of which components above end up in your scope.

## What to ask for specifically

1. Request a line-item breakdown rather than only a total, covering design and build, copy, media handling, SEO groundwork and maintenance.
2. Ask what happens to the price if scope changes mid-project. This is where a headline number most often diverges from the final bill.
3. Compare quotes within the same category rather than measuring a DIY builder against a custom-built studio project as if they're interchangeable.
4. Ask specifically what maintenance costs after launch, since a near-zero number at launch often defers this cost.
5. Weigh a quote's transparency as much as its total. A studio willing to break down the number in detail is telling you something real about how disciplined its process is.

## The honest limits of this

A clear breakdown protects against being surprised by what's missing. It doesn't guarantee a fair price on its own, since a studio can itemise generously and still price each component high. Use the breakdown to compare scope accurately between quotes rather than as a substitute for judging whether the total is fair for what's delivered.

## Frequently asked questions

**What actually drives the cost of a small business website in India?**
Design and build, copywriting, image and media handling, basic SEO groundwork, and ongoing post-launch maintenance. Each can be included, excluded or partial in ways meaningfully changing the total.

**Why can't two websites be fairly compared by their headline price alone?**
The same number can represent very different scopes. One quote might include copywriting and SEO groundwork while another excludes both, shifting real cost onto the client without it appearing as a line item.

**Is a cheaper DIY builder a fair comparison against a custom studio quote?**
Not directly. They're different categories producing different outcomes, and a fair comparison requires accounting for what each actually delivers rather than only their price tags.
