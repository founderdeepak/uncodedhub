*By [Deepak](https://in.linkedin.com/in/deepakdeveloper), co-founder of Uncoded Hub, where he leads sales and strategy. Verified as of September 10, 2026.*

A business doing both modular kitchens and standalone furniture often builds one undifferentiated website for both, and the furniture side quietly underperforms. A kitchen is a whole-room commitment decided once. A dining table is a single, often faster, sometimes even impulse-adjacent purchase. A site built around the kitchen decision process serves the furniture buyer badly.

## The short answer

Furniture buyers move faster, compare more directly on price and material per piece, and often browse rather than commit to a full consultation process the way a kitchen buyer does. A furniture studio's site needs a genuine product-browsing structure with clear individual items, prices and materials, rather than a portfolio-and-consultation model borrowed from the kitchen side of the business.

## Build separate avatars, even within one business

Ryan Deiss and Russ Henneberry's method from [*Digital Marketing For Dummies*](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/33645930-digital-marketing-for-dummies), build a specific, named customer avatar for each distinct segment you serve since broad campaigns aimed at everyone resonate with no one, applies directly to a business selling both categories. The kitchen buyer and the furniture buyer are different people making different kinds of decisions, even when they're sometimes literally the same person at different points. Serving both well means genuinely separate content paths rather than one generic "our work" gallery.

## Redraw the value curve for a faster-moving category

W. Chan Kim and Renée Mauborgne's Four Actions Framework from [*The Blue Ocean Strategy Reader*](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/31213452-the-w-chan-kim-and-ren-e-mauborgne-blue-ocean-strategy-reader), eliminate, reduce, raise and create to redesign your value curve rather than incrementally improving on the default, applied to furniture specifically means eliminating the long consultation-first funnel a kitchen buyer expects. Reducing the emphasis on project timelines and site-visit scheduling. Raising product-level detail, covering dimensions, materials, finish options and price, so it's visible immediately. And creating something the kitchen side doesn't need at all: a genuine browsable catalog structure.

## Own the category distinctly rather than as an extension

Al Ries' rule from [*The 22 Immutable Laws of Marketing*](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/33449.The_22_Immutable_Laws_of_Marketing), own one word in the prospect's mind since a narrow position beats a broad one, is worth applying to how the furniture line is positioned relative to the kitchen business. If the furniture side is treated as an upsell mentioned in passing on the kitchen site, it never gets the chance to be found or considered on its own terms by someone specifically searching for furniture, independent of a kitchen project.

## What a furniture studio's site actually needs

Individual product pages rather than only a project gallery. Dimensions, materials, finish options and price per piece, presented the way a genuine product catalog would be rather than folded into a broader "our work" portfolio.

Faster, lower-friction enquiry paths. A furniture buyer deciding on one piece doesn't need the same qualifying, multi-step process a full kitchen renovation enquiry does. Matching the friction to the size of the decision matters here specifically.

Material and craftsmanship detail per item, applying the same specificity discipline covered in [What Homeowners Look for Before Requesting a Quote](/blog/what-homeowners-look-for-before-requesting-a-quote) for the kitchen side, at the individual product level rather than the whole-project level.

Clear, separate navigation from the kitchen or renovation content, so a furniture-only buyer doesn't wade through unrelated whole-home renovation content to find what they're looking for.

## What to actually do

1. Build genuinely separate content sections for furniture and modular kitchen work rather than one undifferentiated portfolio.
2. Create individual product pages for your furniture line covering dimensions, materials and price, rather than only project photos.
3. Simplify the enquiry path for furniture-only interest, matching friction to the size of the decision.
4. Position the furniture line as its own offer, findable independently rather than only as a mention within kitchen project content.
5. Check your navigation. Can a visitor interested only in furniture find what they need without wading through kitchen-specific content?

## The honest limits of this

Separating these two offers takes real structural work, and for a very small business maintaining two genuinely distinct content paths may not be worth the effort relative to demand. This is a change worth making once furniture is a real, standalone revenue line rather than a minor add-on to the kitchen business.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why can't a furniture studio use the same website structure as a modular kitchen business?**
The buyer decisions are genuinely different. Furniture is often a faster, more product-comparison-driven purchase, while a kitchen is a whole-room commitment typically decided through a longer consultation process. A site built for one serves the other badly.

**What does a furniture studio's website need that a kitchen-focused site doesn't?**
Individual product pages with dimensions, materials and pricing, a faster and lower-friction enquiry path, and clear separation from unrelated whole-home renovation content.

**Should a business selling both offer them on completely separate websites?**
Not necessarily. Clearly separated sections or paths within one site usually work well enough, as long as a furniture-only visitor can navigate to relevant content without friction.
