A studio's homepage features a serene living room with velvet cushions and ambient brass lighting. Right next to it is a thirty-seater corporate cafeteria and an IT server room.

The founder believes this proves versatility. *"We can do anything from a cozy 3BHK to a corporate headquarters."*

In reality, it proves to both buyers that you are not a specialist. 

The homeowner building a luxury villa in Koramangala looks at the office cubicles and worries your residential work will feel sterile and institutional. Meanwhile, the corporate procurement manager looking to fit out a 20,000 sq ft office in Whitefield looks at the velvet cushions and concludes you do not understand commercial HVAC loads, fire evacuation codes, or corporate penalty clauses.

## The short answer

Residential interior design is an emotional, personal investment paid out of personal savings. Commercial fit-outs are risk-mitigation procurement decisions evaluated on commercial lease timelines, statutory compliance, acoustic certifications, and cost per desk. Putting both on a single generic website dilutes your positioning. If you serve both markets, create strictly separated site sections with dedicated navigation paths, proof assets, and inquiry flows.

## How residential and commercial decision-makers evaluate websites

In [*Never Split the Difference*](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/26156469-never-split-the-difference), Chris Voss notes that people do not make decisions based on logic; they make decisions based on risk avoidance, then rationalize them with logic later.

The risks faced by a residential client and a commercial buyer are entirely different.

| Evaluation Metric | Luxury Residential Client | Corporate Commercial Facility Lead |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Primary Fear** | Being overcharged; poor material durability; ugly aesthetic result | Project delivery delays resulting in dead rent; fire NOC rejection |
| **Decision-Maker** | Husband and wife / family consensus | Facility Director, CFO, Procurement Committee |
| **Proof Required** | Editorial photography, client lifestyle quotes, spatial elegance | Safety compliance certifications, vendor balance sheet, handover SLAs |
| **Key Cost Metric** | Lump-sum total investment / finish quality | Fit-out cost per sq ft / cost per employee workstation |
| **Timeline Pressure** | "We hope to move in before the festival" | "Our lease commences on October 1st; delays cost ₹8 Lakhs daily" |

## The architectural problem with combined portfolios

When a website displays residential and commercial projects in a single mixed grid, navigation breaks down. 

As we covered in [[04 - 7 Website Mistakes That Make a Design Studio Look Smaller]], ambiguous positioning makes a firm look like a collection of freelancers taking whatever work walks in the door, rather than an established enterprise.

If an enterprise client visits your site, they need to see:
- Workstation densities and circulation plans
- HVAC, electrical, and data cabling integration
- Fire-retardant material specifications
- LEED / green building acoustic ratings
- Case studies detailing phased fit-outs in occupied buildings

None of this information belongs in front of a homeowner deciding whether to put fluted panels behind their master bed.

## How to structure a dual-practice website cleanly

If your firm genuinely handles both practices at scale, you have three viable architectural options:

### Option 1: The Gateway Split (Recommended for equal revenue practices)
On the root homepage, present two clear, authoritative doorways:
- Residential Studio: High-end homes, penthouses, bespoke villas.
- Commercial & Workplace: Corporate offices, retail spaces, hospitality.

Clicking either path tailors the entire navigation, case study library, and inquiry form to that specific buyer. The residential side links to home lifestyle questionnaires; the commercial side provides downloadable capability decks and vendor registration details.

### Option 2: Dedicated Sub-Domains or Separate Brands
For large studios, spinning off commercial fit-outs under a distinct commercial brand (e.g. *Studio Name Workplaces*) is often the cleanest move. Corporate facility heads prefer dealing with commercial-only fit-out contractors who carry commercial liability insurance and formal health and safety policies.

### Option 3: Dominant Practice with Secondary Nav
If residential represents eighty percent of your revenue, your main site should be unapologetically residential. Place commercial projects under a secondary header link (*"Workplace & Retail"*), keeping the homepage focused on high-ticket homeowners.

## Tailoring the Call to Action

A residential client wants to schedule a design consultation or explore project scopes as described in [[11 - Interior Design Client Questionnaire Website]]. 

A commercial facility director wants an RFP submission portal, a meeting with a project director, or a formal company profile download. Forcing a commercial lead through an eight-step home renovation form is an instant bounce.

Make the commercial contact path direct:
- Submit RFP / Tender documents
- Download Vendor Pre-Qualification Deck
- Direct line to Commercial Practice Lead

Separating these paths preserves the premium aura of your residential work while giving corporate decision-makers the rigorous operational data they need to approve your vendor bid.
