Someone reads a genuinely good LinkedIn post. It lands. They click through to the profile, then click the link in bio.

What they land on is either a Linktree with five undifferentiated buttons, covering newsletter, book a call, free guide, Instagram and podcast, with no context for which one to pick, or a real page continuing exactly the conversation the post started. The click either compounds the work that post did or wastes it. Most coaching and consulting websites waste it. We don't coach or consult ourselves, but the pattern here is the same one we've studied across every relationship-driven niche we build for: the follower already believes something. The website's only job is not to waste that.

## The short answer

A coach or consultant's website should include who you coach and what specific transformation you deliver, stated in language matching your LinkedIn voice. The core belief your work challenges, made explicit. A visible path from free to paid to high-ticket. Specific outcome-based proof rather than generic praise. A plainly described process for what working together looks like. And one call to action, a discovery call, with every competing link removed.

## Why a LinkedIn profile alone isn't the same as an authority website

A LinkedIn profile is rented space. You can build a following there, and the algorithm, the layout and the platform's own priorities aren't yours to control. A profile visitor sees you exactly the way every other coach on the platform is seen. Same layout, same About box, same constraints.

Donald Miller's rule in [*Building a StoryBrand*](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/34460583-building-a-storybrand) is where most authority sites go wrong in the opposite direction from other niches. Coaches over-correct into making the website entirely about themselves, their journey and their credentials, when the visitor arrived wanting to know one thing: does this person understand my specific problem well enough to help me solve it.

Russell Brunson's concept of the Attractive Character from [*DotCom Secrets*](https://www.goodreads.com/en/book/show/25054961-dotcom-secrets) is useful framing here, used honestly. People follow people rather than logos, and your website is where the persona your audience already follows on LinkedIn gets a home that isn't rented, with room to make an actual case rather than a feed-sized fragment of one.

## What should a coach's website include?

**1. Who you coach, and what specific transformation, in your own LinkedIn voice**

Not "I help people unlock their potential". Something as specific and repeatable as your LinkedIn positioning already is. A visitor arriving from a post should feel zero discontinuity between what they just read and what they're now looking at.

**2. The core belief your work challenges, made explicit**

Brunson's framing in [*Expert Secrets*](https://www.goodreads.com/en/book/show/35254158-expert-secrets), that every offer needs a Big Domino, one false belief that once knocked down makes the sale inevitable, is a genuinely useful diagnostic for a homepage when used honestly rather than as a manipulation tactic. What does your ideal client currently believe that's wrong, and that your work corrects? Naming it plainly does more work than a list of credentials.

**3. A visible path from free to paid to high-ticket**

Brunson's Value Ladder concept from the same book, a sequence of offers from free or cheap through to high-ticket with each solving the problem the last one created, gives a cold visitor a way to self-select their entry point rather than being shown one all-or-nothing offer that may not match where they are.

**4. Specific, outcome-based proof**

Andy Maslen's rule holds here as everywhere: one or three testimonials, in the client's own words, unpolished. "Changed my life" convinces nobody. A testimonial naming the actual before-state, the actual after-state, and the real timeframe does the work.

**5. A plainly described process**

What working together actually looks like. Session structure, frequency, duration, what's expected of the client between sessions. Miller's three-steps-or-fewer plan applies directly, since uncertainty about the mechanics of coaching is one of the quieter reasons a warm lead never books the call.

**6. One call to action, with every competing link removed**

This is the direct fix for the Linktree problem in the opening story. "Book a discovery call", repeated, with nothing else pulling attention away.

**7. A content hub extending your LinkedIn thinking**

Ann Handley's rule from [*Content Rules*](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/8102305-content-rules), share or solve rather than shill, is exactly what a coach's blog or resource section should do. Take the ideas working on LinkedIn and give them room to be fully argued, which is also the content that ranks in search and gets cited by AI assistants long after the original post has scrolled out of anyone's feed.

**8. Real credibility signals, nothing fabricated**

Media features, actual client logos with permission, genuine certifications. Never inflated numbers or invented client counts. In a niche built entirely on trust, one exaggeration discovered later costs more than a hundred honest posts earn.

**9. Speed on mobile**

LinkedIn traffic arrives disproportionately on phones during a scroll break. A slow-loading site loses exactly the visitor a good post just warmed up.

## Do consultants need a website if they already have LinkedIn?

Yes, for a reason specific to this niche. LinkedIn is where the relationship starts and where the content building trust lives day to day. A website is where that trust gets a place to compound. It's searchable, citable by AI assistants when someone asks for a coach in your niche, and it's the one asset a platform algorithm change can't quietly take away from you.

## Linktree versus a full authority website. Which should the bio link point to?

A Linktree is a router. It holds five links. It can't hold the Big Domino argument, can't be found in Google search, and can't be the thing an AI assistant cites when someone asks for a coach who works with people like them. A real website does all three. The honest trade-off is that a Linktree takes ten minutes to set up and a proper site takes real work, though the router only ever routes and never persuades.

## How does a website help a coach convert LinkedIn followers into clients?

By giving the follower a next step better than "keep scrolling and hope you post again". The website is where a LinkedIn follower becomes a discovery-call booking, which is a different conversion event from a LinkedIn comment or a DM, and one a feed post by design can't host on its own.

## I'm a business coach active on LinkedIn. What should my website include to convert profile visitors into discovery calls?

1. Match your homepage headline to your actual LinkedIn positioning, with zero discontinuity between the post and the click.
2. Name the one false belief your work corrects, plainly, near the top of the page.
3. Add a visible free-to-paid path so a cold visitor can self-select an entry point.
4. Replace generic testimonials with two or three specific, outcome-based ones.
5. Write out your actual process, covering session structure, frequency and what's expected of the client.
6. Remove every link competing with "Book a discovery call".
7. Add two or three pieces of content extending a LinkedIn idea into something fully argued and searchable.

## The honest limits of this

A website won't build the LinkedIn following sending it traffic in the first place. That's a separate, ongoing effort. It won't fix a positioning that isn't already landing with your LinkedIn audience, since a confusing website on top of confusing content is still confusing. And a Big Domino that isn't genuinely true reads as manipulative the moment a prospective client gets on a call and finds the reality doesn't match the page.
