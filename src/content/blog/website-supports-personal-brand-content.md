A coach posts consistently on LinkedIn for six months. Good engagement, real comments, occasional DMs. At the end of it they have a following and almost nothing to show for it beyond the platform itself. Every insight they shared is buried in a feed nobody can search, effectively gone the moment it scrolled past.

The website isn't a competing project to that six months of work. It's the missing place where that work was supposed to accumulate.

## The short answer

A coach's website is where LinkedIn content compounds instead of disappearing. LinkedIn builds the relationship in the moment. A website turns the best of that thinking into something searchable, citable by AI assistants, and independent of any single platform's feed algorithm.

## What happens to a good LinkedIn post after 48 hours

Almost nothing. The feed algorithm has decided its reach, engagement has settled, and within a few days it's effectively invisible to anyone who wasn't following at the exact moment it published. That isn't a flaw in LinkedIn. It's what a feed is for. Simon Sinek's distinction in [*Start With Why*](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/7108725-start-with-why) is useful here: manipulations lead to transactions while inspiration leads to loyalty, and a feed by its nature optimises for the transaction of the scroll rather than the long-term accumulation of trust in an idea.

A website doesn't have this problem. A page built around the same idea a LinkedIn post made keeps being findable, by search, by a direct link, or by an AI assistant answering a question about coaching a year from now, long after the post itself vanished into the feed's history.

## Ryan Holiday's frame, applied to a coach's content

[*Perennial Seller*](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/32968546-perennial-seller) draws a sharp distinction between a short promotional burst and a long, patient platform-building process judged over years rather than by opening-week numbers. LinkedIn content, by its cadence, sits structurally closer to the promotional burst. Daily or near-daily, optimised for the current moment's algorithm. A website's content doesn't need that cadence. It needs to be right once, and then it keeps working. These aren't competing time investments. They're the same underlying thinking running on two very different clocks.

## The specific mechanism: reimagine rather than recycle

Ann Handley's rule in [*Content Rules*](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/8102305-content-rules), reimagine rather than recycle by repurposing existing content into new formats and angles instead of re-posting the same thing everywhere, describes exactly how the two should connect. A LinkedIn post generating genuine engagement is a signal rather than a finished product. It's telling you which idea landed. The website's job is taking that validated idea and giving it the room a 200-word post never had: the full argument, the counter-objections, the worked example, the specific outcome.

This is also where Handley's Content Circle of Life, a real publishing schedule mixing formats rather than relying on one channel, becomes practical rather than aspirational. LinkedIn posts feed the website, the website's deeper pieces get re-cut into new LinkedIn posts, and the cycle compounds instead of restarting from zero every week.

## Process rather than product, applied to authority-building

[*Show Your Work!*](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/18290401-show-your-work)'s core argument, that documenting how you make things is itself valuable content separate from the finished output, is already how most coaches instinctively use LinkedIn. Sharing the thinking as it develops rather than only the polished conclusion. The gap is that this process documentation has nowhere permanent to live. A resource section or blog on the website is where a scattered year of "here's what I'm learning" posts becomes a coherent body of work a new visitor can walk through in order, rather than reconstructing it by scrolling a stranger's feed history.

## What breaks when the two aren't connected

A visitor who finds a great LinkedIn post and clicks through to a website with nothing related to what they just read experiences the discontinuity described in [What a Coach's Website Should Include](/blog/what-a-coachs-website-should-include). The click wastes the post's work instead of extending it. The fix isn't complicated. The website's homepage and content should sound like the same person who wrote the post, making the same kind of case, in the same voice, with more room to make it.

## What to do about this, practically

1. Pull your three best-performing LinkedIn posts from the last few months, meaning the ones that got real engagement rather than only likes.
2. Turn each into a full page or article on your website, with the argument fully made rather than compressed into a feed-sized fragment.
3. Link back to those pages the next time you post something related on LinkedIn, closing the loop rather than leaving it one-directional.
4. Check that your homepage voice matches your LinkedIn voice. A visitor arriving from a post should feel zero discontinuity.
5. Stop treating the website as a separate project. It's the same content system running on a different clock.

## The honest limits of this

A website full of repurposed LinkedIn content still needs the LinkedIn presence generating new ideas and new traffic. This isn't a way to stop posting. And repurposing only works if the original posts were genuinely good, since turning a weak post into a longer weak page just makes it longer.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does a coach's website compete with their LinkedIn content, or support it?**
It supports it. LinkedIn builds the relationship in real time, and the website is where the best of that thinking compounds into something searchable and permanent instead of disappearing after 48 hours.

**What should go on a coach's website that isn't just repurposed LinkedIn content?**
The fuller argument a post never had room for, covering counter-objections, a worked example and a specific outcome, built from the LinkedIn posts that already proved an idea resonates.

**Should a coach stop posting on LinkedIn once they have a good website?**
No. The website depends on LinkedIn to keep generating both new ideas and new traffic. The two are one content system on two different clocks.
