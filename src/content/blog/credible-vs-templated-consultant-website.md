*By [Deepak](https://in.linkedin.com/in/deepakdeveloper), co-founder of Uncoded Hub, where he leads sales and strategy. Verified as of September 10, 2026.*

Load ten coaching websites in a row and a pattern appears fast. The same hero layout, the same stock photo of a confident person mid-gesture, the same headline shape of "Helping [audience] achieve [outcome]" with only the nouns swapped. None of them are badly built. All of them are instantly forgettable, because they're built from the same template with different words dropped in.

A prospective client can't articulate exactly why one of these feels trustworthy and the rest feel interchangeable. They can feel it within seconds.

## The short answer

Credibility comes from specificity a template can't fake. A genuinely narrow positioning rather than a broad one. Real outcomes described in detail rather than vague promises. A voice sounding like one specific person rather than a category of person. And proof that couldn't have been copy-pasted from a competitor's site. Templated design isn't really a visual problem. It's a content problem wearing a visual symptom.

## Own one word, not a whole category

Al Ries' rule from [*The 22 Immutable Laws of Marketing*](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/33449.The_22_Immutable_Laws_of_Marketing), own one word in the prospect's mind since a narrow position beats a broad one, explains most of the interchangeability problem directly. "I help entrepreneurs grow their business" is a category with thousands of entrants and zero differentiation. A consultant genuinely narrow enough to name a specific audience, a specific problem and a specific timeframe has already done more to feel credible than any amount of design polish could achieve on top of a generic position.

## Generic copy breaks a norm the whole relationship depends on

Dan Ariely's finding in [*Predictably Irrational*](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/1713426.Predictably_Irrational), that social norms and market norms don't mix and introducing an obviously transactional, salesy tone into what should feel like a genuine relationship can permanently damage trust rather than only change its terms, describes why templated marketing copy reads as a warning sign in this niche. Coaching and consulting run entirely on a relationship of trust. The moment a website's language starts sounding like a sales funnel template rather than a specific person talking, it quietly signals market norms, which sits uneasily against the social norm of trust a coaching relationship needs from the first click.

## Genuine interest reads differently from generic promises

Dale Carnegie's principle from [*How to Win Friends & Influence People*](https://www.goodreads.com/en/book/show/4865.How_to_Win_Friends_Influence_People), become genuinely interested in other people before trying to get them interested in you, is a useful test to apply to your own homepage copy. Does it spend its first few lines on the visitor's specific situation, or does it open with your own credentials and journey? A templated site almost always defaults to the latter, because "about me" content is easier to write in the abstract than content demonstrating real understanding of one specific kind of person's specific problem.

## What actually signals real

Language only this person would use. Not "I empower clients to unlock their potential", but the actual phrases, framing and specific worldview coming from real practice. The kind that couldn't be lifted and dropped onto a competitor's site without sounding wrong there.

Outcomes described with real texture. Not "clients see incredible results", but a specific situation and a specific shift, described with enough detail that it couldn't have been invented. This is the same specificity rule governing testimonials everywhere else in this content plan, applied to how you describe your own work.

A process sounding like it's actually been run before. Vague promises read as templated. A described sequence covering what session one looks like and what changes by week four reads as something genuinely repeated with real clients, because it's too specific to have been invented for the page.

Evidence that couldn't be copy-pasted. A client's name, a specific detail about their situation, a real screenshot or quote. Anything a competitor literally could not lift and use on their own site because it belongs uniquely to this practice.

## What templated design gets wrong beyond the copy

Visually, the tell is usually the same hero pattern repeated everywhere. Full-width photo, centred headline, a single generic CTA button. A layout so common it's stopped registering as a choice. This isn't inherently bad design. The problem is that when the copy above it is also generic, the whole page reads as assembled rather than built, and a visitor's instinct for having seen this exact page shape a hundred times fires before they've read a word.

## What to actually fix

1. Rewrite your positioning statement until it names a specific audience and a specific problem, narrow enough to sound wrong applied to a different kind of coach.
2. Replace any generic outcome language with one real, textured example per major claim.
3. Read your homepage's first three lines and check whether they're about the visitor's situation or your own credentials. Flip the order if it's the latter.
4. Add one piece of genuinely unique evidence, whether a real client detail or a specific process step, that couldn't be copy-pasted onto a competitor's site.
5. Check your process section describes a real sequence rather than a vague promise of transformation.

## The honest limits of this

Specificity alone doesn't build a practice. The positioning still has to be true, and the outcomes described still have to be ones you've actually produced. A narrow, specific-sounding claim not backed by real work is worse than a generic one, because it reads as credible right up until a prospective client asks a follow-up question the site can't honestly answer.

## Frequently asked questions

**What makes a coach's website look credible instead of generic?**
A genuinely narrow positioning rather than a broad one, specific and textured outcome descriptions rather than vague promises, and evidence that couldn't be copy-pasted onto a competitor's site.

**Is templated design itself the problem, or is it the copy?**
Mostly the copy. A common layout isn't inherently a problem. It becomes one when the language on top of it is also generic, and the combination reads as assembled rather than built.

**How narrow should a coach's positioning be to feel credible?**
Narrow enough that the statement would sound wrong applied to a different kind of coach. If a competitor could paste your positioning onto their site without anything sounding off, it isn't narrow enough yet.
