*By [Geetha](https://www.linkedin.com/in/geethaspecialist), co-founder of Uncoded Hub, where she leads project delivery. Verified as of September 10, 2026.*

A coach compares a cheap Linktree-plus-landing-page setup against a proper authority site and picks the cheaper option, reasoning that a simple link page does most of the same job for a fraction of the cost. It doesn't, and the gap only becomes visible months later once the cheaper setup has quietly failed to compound the way a real site would have.

## The short answer

A coach's website cost depends on what it needs to do rather than a headline figure. A simple landing page is genuinely cheap because it's genuinely limited. A real authority site with a content hub, a described process and a visible value ladder costs more because it's doing meaningfully more work. Rather than comparing prices, the more useful comparison is capability against timeline: what's included, and how fast it's delivered once scope is agreed.

## Anchor on capability rather than the cheapest option available

Dan Ariely's finding in [*Predictably Irrational*](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/1713426.Predictably_Irrational), we rarely judge value in absolute terms and instead compare against whatever reference point is nearby, explains why a Linktree-based setup makes a proper site look expensive even though the two aren't the same category of product. A landing page router and a searchable, content-holding authority site do genuinely different jobs, covered in [What a Coach's Website Should Include](/blog/what-a-coachs-website-should-include). Comparing their prices without comparing their capability is comparing across categories never meant to be judged by the same number.

## What actually drives the scope

Content depth. A site built to hold real, extended arguments, meaning the content-hub function covered in [How a Website Supports the Personal Brand You're Already Building](/blog/website-supports-personal-brand-content), is genuinely more work than a single landing page because there's more to build and write.

A described process and value ladder. Structuring a visible path from free content through paid offerings, per the Value Ladder concept covered in [What a Coach's Website Should Include](/blog/what-a-coachs-website-should-include), is real, deliberate architecture work rather than a page template.

Ongoing content capability. Whether the coach can add new content themselves without going back to a developer changes the total cost of ownership meaningfully, even when it doesn't appear in the initial scope.

Positioning and copywriting depth. The specificity separating a credible site from a templated one, per [What Makes a Consultant's Website Look Credible vs Templated](/blog/credible-vs-templated-consultant-website), often takes as much real work as the site's structure.

## A documented system is what makes a timeline defensible

The core argument of [*The E-Myth Revisited*](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/81948.The_E_myth_Revisited), build your business as a documented, replicable system, applies to how a web design studio should justify its own promise to you. A studio that can explain exactly what a given scope includes, broken into real components, and deliver it on a fixed timeline, is one whose word you can trust. We build every project, regardless of niche, on a fixed seven-day delivery timeline once scope is locked, and if we're late, you don't pay. Ask any studio, us included, to explain what has to be true on your side for that clock to actually hold.

## Define the market narrowly before assuming a generic scope applies

Peter Thiel's framing from [*Zero to One*](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/18050143-zero-to-one), define your market narrowly enough to actually dominate it rather than disguising a crowded category as unique, is worth applying to how you shop for this. "Website for a coach" isn't one product. A coach launching their first offer needs something very different from an established consultant with years of client history and a defined value ladder already running. Comparing quotes without first being specific about which of these you are produces mismatched, unhelpful comparisons.

## What to actually do before getting quotes

1. Decide honestly which stage you're at, whether first offer or established practice with a real value ladder, before comparing scopes.
2. Ask any quote what it includes specifically, covering content depth, process and value-ladder structuring, and ongoing self-editing capability.
3. Compare capability rather than only price between a landing-page router and a real authority site, since they aren't the same category.
4. Ask what happens if you want to add content yourself later, since this affects the real cost of ownership beyond the initial scope.
5. Ask what has to be true on your side, meaning content and decisions ready in advance, for a fixed delivery timeline to actually hold.

## The honest limits of this

There's no single defensible number for a coach's website applying across every stage and scope, because the honest answer depends entirely on what you need it to do. What we can state plainly rather than guess at is the timeline: once scope and positioning are locked, delivery is seven days, and late means free.

## Frequently asked questions

**How much should a coach's website cost?**
It depends on scope rather than a single figure. A simple landing page does meaningfully less than a real authority site with content depth and a described value ladder. Compare capability and delivery timeline alongside price.

**Why isn't a cheap Linktree-plus-landing-page setup a fair comparison against a full authority site?**
They're different categories doing different jobs, a router versus a searchable, content-holding asset. Comparing their prices without accounting for what each produces misses the real decision.

**What should a coach ask before getting a website quote?**
What's actually included, covering content depth, process structuring and whether you can add content yourself later, and what needs to be ready on your side for a fixed delivery timeline to hold.
