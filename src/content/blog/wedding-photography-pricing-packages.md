*By [Deepak](https://in.linkedin.com/in/deepakdeveloper), co-founder of Uncoded Hub, where he leads sales and strategy. Verified as of September 10, 2026.*

A three-tier pricing table, Basic, Standard, Premium, each with a bullet list of what's included, is honest and clear. It also flattens a deeply personal purchase into something reading like a phone plan. The information is right. The presentation undersells what's being sold.

## The short answer

Presenting packages well means giving each tier a real point of view rather than a feature checklist, using deliberate anchoring so the middle option reads as the obvious choice, and framing the differences around the experience and coverage a couple cares about rather than hour counts and deliverable numbers.

## Anchoring: the middle tier is designed rather than neutral

Dan Ariely's finding in [*Predictably Irrational*](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/1713426.Predictably_Irrational), we rarely judge value in absolute terms and instead compare against whatever reference point is nearby, is the mechanic behind why most businesses present three tiers rather than one price. The highest tier isn't only for the couples who'll buy it. It also makes the middle tier look reasonable by comparison. Ordering and spacing this deliberately, rather than listing three options as if equally weighted, is what makes a pricing table work rather than only inform.

## Give copy a real reason to act rather than a feature list

The core discipline from [*Ready, Fire, Aim*](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/2490652.Ready_Fire_Aim), build a Unique Selling Proposition and make it the organising principle for all your marketing copy rather than an afterthought, is what most wedding photography pricing tables skip. "8 hours coverage, 400 edited photos, online gallery" is a feature list. "Full-day coverage means we're there for the quiet moments before anyone's ready, not just the ceremony onward" is the same information organised around what the couple is buying: the story rather than the line items.

## Make the benefit explicit, per tier

Karl Blanks' rule from [*Making Websites Win*](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/39668820-making-websites-win), make benefits explicit and concrete because visitors won't infer value, applies to the gap between what most tiers list and what a couple needs to know to choose. A bullet list of hours and photo counts requires the couple to infer what it means for their day. A sentence naming what each tier actually covers in real terms, such as "enough time for a second outfit and golden-hour portraits" rather than only "10 hours", does that inference work for them.

## What makes tiers read as considered rather than templated

A real name and identity per tier rather than only Basic, Standard and Premium. Something reflecting what the package is for, even as simple as naming it by what it includes rather than a generic label.

One sentence of real context per tier, explaining what the difference means for the couple's day rather than a longer feature list.

Deliberate visual weighting toward the tier you want chosen most, rather than presenting three options with identical visual emphasis.

Honest inclusions and exclusions, stated plainly. The specificity discipline running through this entire content plan applies here too. Vague inclusions such as "professional editing" mean less than specific ones such as "every photo colour-graded, top selects chosen and delivered within a stated timeframe".

## The honest tension with pricing transparency

There's a real trade-off worth naming. Too much granular detail in a public pricing table reads as overly transactional for a purchase this personal, while too little leaves a couple guessing. The resolution isn't hiding pricing. It's presenting it with the same narrative care given to the rest of the site rather than switching into a completely different, colder register the moment pricing appears.

## What to actually do

1. Give each tier a real name and one sentence of context rather than only a feature checklist.
2. Reorder and visually weight your tiers so the one you want most couples to choose reads as the obvious middle ground.
3. Rewrite feature bullets into benefit statements naming what each inclusion means for the couple's day.
4. Keep the same voice across your pricing section that runs through the rest of your site, rather than switching into a colder register because numbers appear.
5. Test the page with someone who's never seen it and ask which tier they'd pick and why. If the answer doesn't match your intended anchor, the presentation needs adjusting rather than the prices.

## The honest limits of this

Better presentation makes pricing feel more considered. It doesn't make an unreasonably priced package attractive, and a couple genuinely outside your price range won't be persuaded by better copy alone. This is about presenting real value clearly rather than disguising a mismatch between price and what's delivered.

## Frequently asked questions

**How should wedding photography pricing tiers be presented to avoid looking like a menu?**
Give each tier a real name and context sentence rather than only a feature checklist, use deliberate visual weighting to guide toward a preferred tier, and keep the same narrative voice used throughout the rest of the site.

**Why does the highest-priced tier matter even if few couples choose it?**
Anchoring. A genuinely premium top tier makes the middle option look more reasonable by comparison, which is a large part of why most pricing pages use three tiers rather than one.

**Is it better to list exact deliverables or describe the experience for each tier?**
Both, ideally. Specific deliverables build trust through honesty, while a sentence describing what that means for the couple's day does the inference work a bare feature list leaves to the reader.
