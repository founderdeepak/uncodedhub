*By [Geetha](https://www.linkedin.com/in/geethaspecialist), co-founder of Uncoded Hub, where she leads project delivery. Verified as of September 10, 2026.*

A small builder posts project updates to a Facebook page for years. Photos of progress, handover celebrations, the occasional inventory announcement. It works, in the sense that existing followers see it. What it does nothing for is the buyer who's never heard of the builder and is searching Google for "builders in [area]" right now, ready to shortlist someone.

## The short answer

A Facebook page reaches people who already follow you, and an algorithm decides how much of even that audience sees each post. A website is searchable by strangers with active buying intent, holds detailed project documentation a feed post can't, and is the asset a bank, an approval authority or a cautious buyer expects to find when verifying a builder is a real, established business.

## Rented reach versus owned infrastructure

Damian Ryan's distinction in [*Understanding Digital Marketing*](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/18405805-understanding-digital-marketing) is the cleanest way to see the gap: categorise every marketing effort as owned, paid or earned media, and track each differently. A Facebook page is functionally rented. The platform decides reach, can change the algorithm's rules without notice, and every post's visibility depends on factors outside the builder's control. A website is owned media. The builder controls what's on it, how it's structured, and whether it ranks in search, with no platform able to quietly throttle that reach.

## The documented-system argument

The core argument of [*The E-Myth Revisited*](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/81948.The_E_myth_Revisited) is to build your business as a documented, replicable system, a franchise prototype, even if you never plan to franchise it. That translates into credibility as much as operations. A builder's website documenting project history, RERA registration details, completed handover timelines and floor plans in a structured, permanent way reads as an established, systemised business. A Facebook feed of scattered updates, however genuine, reads as informal, even for a builder whose actual operations are highly disciplined.

## What a buyer specifically needs to verify

Property buyers doing real diligence on a small builder look for things a Facebook page structurally can't hold well. RERA registration and project approval details. A clear record of past projects with handover dates. Floor plans and specifications for current inventory. A way to reach the right person directly rather than commenting on a post and hoping for a reply. None of this is impossible on Facebook. It's just poorly suited to the format, buried in old posts, hard to search, and easy to miss.

## The bank and institutional angle

A meaningfully different audience also needs a real website: banks processing home loans, approval authorities, and larger institutional buyers evaluating a builder for bulk purchase or partnership. None of these audiences treat a Facebook page as a credible substitute for a proper business presence. For a small builder trying to be taken seriously alongside larger developers, this gap matters as much as the retail buyer's search behaviour.

## What Facebook still does well

Facebook groups genuinely outperform many other channels for reaching an older, more patient, higher-net-worth local audience, and a Facebook page's low-friction update format suits ongoing progress photos existing followers want to see. The realistic system is Facebook for ongoing community engagement with people who already know you, and a website for the buyer, bank or institution encountering you for the first time.

## What to actually build

1. Publish a structured project page for every current and past project, covering RERA details, floor plans and handover history, rather than scattering them across old Facebook posts.
2. Make sure your RERA registration and approvals are easy to find rather than something a buyer has to ask for.
3. Add a direct contact path that doesn't depend on a buyer commenting on a post and waiting.
4. Keep the Facebook page running for existing followers and community engagement.
5. Check what a bank or institutional buyer would find searching your builder name, and make sure a real website is what they land on.

## The honest limits of this

A website doesn't replace the trust a builder has already built with an existing local audience through years of visible Facebook updates. That audience should keep being served the way it already is. What a website adds is reach to everyone outside it: strangers searching, banks verifying, institutions evaluating, none of whom a Facebook page was built to reach.

## Frequently asked questions

**What does a small builder's website do that a Facebook page can't?**
It's searchable by buyers with no prior awareness of the builder, holds structured project and approval documentation permanently, and is what banks and institutional buyers expect to find when verifying credibility. A Facebook page is poorly suited to all three.

**Should a small builder stop using Facebook if they build a website?**
No. Facebook remains effective for engaging an existing, already-following local audience. The two serve different audiences, one for people who already know you and one for people encountering you for the first time.

**Why do banks and institutions specifically need a real website rather than a Facebook page?**
Verifying a builder's legitimacy, covering RERA registration, project history and approvals, requires structured, permanent documentation that a social feed isn't built to hold or make findable.
