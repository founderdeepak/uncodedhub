*By [Geetha](https://www.linkedin.com/in/geethaspecialist), co-founder of Uncoded Hub, where she leads project delivery. Verified as of September 10, 2026.*

A modular interior design business copies the bespoke-studio playbook. A portfolio of dreamy finished spaces, a philosophy statement, minimal pricing information. Then it wonders why enquiries stall at "just checking rates". The buyer looking for a modular, catalog-based solution isn't shopping the way a bespoke client is, and a site built for one doesn't convert the other.

## The short answer

A bespoke studio sells a custom creative process. A modular business sells a repeatable, priced system. The website has to match which one it actually is. Modular buyers want configuration options, transparent pricing and speed, rather than a portfolio built around artistic narrative. Copying a bespoke studio's site structure onto a modular business quietly signals a slower, pricier process than the business actually offers.

## Redraw the value curve instead of copying the category default

W. Chan Kim and Renée Mauborgne's Four Actions Framework from [*The Blue Ocean Strategy Reader*](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/31213452-the-w-chan-kim-and-ren-e-mauborgne-blue-ocean-strategy-reader) asks you to eliminate, reduce, raise and create in order to redesign your value curve, rather than incrementally improving on the existing industry offer. Applied to a modular business's website, that means eliminating the philosophy-and-artist-statement content a bespoke site leads with, reducing the amount of unpriced ambiguous portfolio browsing, raising visible configuration options and pricing transparency and speed-to-quote, and creating something a bespoke competitor's site doesn't have at all: a real, usable online configurator or price calculator. Copying the bespoke playbook and changing the photos misses the differentiation actually available here.

## Build for the buyer who's comparing you

Ryan Deiss and Russ Henneberry's method in [*Digital Marketing For Dummies*](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/33645930-digital-marketing-for-dummies) surfaces the real difference in buyer behaviour: build a specific, named customer avatar, because broad campaigns aimed at everyone resonate with no one. A bespoke client is comparing creative vision and trust. A modular buyer is comparing price, speed and configuration options, often against a handful of other modular providers in a fairly direct, feature-by-feature way. A site written for the bespoke buyer's emotional decision process doesn't answer the modular buyer's practical comparison questions.

## Own a distinct category rather than a diluted version of "interior design"

Al Ries' rule from [*The 22 Immutable Laws of Marketing*](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/33449.The_22_Immutable_Laws_of_Marketing) is to own one word in the prospect's mind, since a narrow position beats a broad one. It applies here in an unusual direction. A modular business trying to look like a bespoke studio is blurring its position rather than narrowing it. It reads as neither clearly affordable and fast nor clearly premium and custom, and a blurred position converts worse than either clear one would on its own.

## What the site actually needs instead

Visible configuration or package options come first, replacing a single undifferentiated portfolio gallery. Modular buyers are comparing specific options, and a site that makes those easy to browse converts better than one requiring a call just to find out what's available.

Transparent, structured pricing comes next, closer to a product catalog than to a bespoke studio's soft price signal. The modular buyer's whole reason for choosing this category over a custom studio is often speed and predictability, and vague pricing undermines the exact thing they came for.

Speed deserves to be an explicit selling point. If turnaround is genuinely faster than a bespoke alternative, state it plainly and early. It's one of the strongest differentiators available and one most modular businesses undersell by copying bespoke messaging.

Finally, proof of consistency rather than individual project beauty. Testimonials and examples that show the modular system delivers reliably across many homes are a different kind of proof from a single stunning bespoke project photo, and for this buyer a more relevant one.

## What to actually do

1. Run the eliminate/reduce/raise/create exercise on your current site against a bespoke competitor's, and note what you're copying that doesn't fit your actual offer.
2. Add a visible configuration or package browser if one doesn't already exist.
3. Make pricing genuinely transparent, closer to a catalog than a soft consultation-based range.
4. State your speed advantage explicitly if you have one. This is one of the most underused differentiators in the category.
5. Rebuild your proof section around consistency across many projects rather than individual beauty shots.

## The honest limits of this

A clearer, more differentiated site attracts the right modular buyer faster. It won't create demand for modular interior design in a market still mostly hiring bespoke studios, and it can't make a genuinely inconsistent product or service look consistent through content alone.

## Frequently asked questions

**Should a modular interior design business copy a bespoke studio's website structure?**
No, because the buyers shop differently. A modular buyer wants configuration options, transparent pricing and speed, while a bespoke studio's site is built around creative narrative and soft pricing, which doesn't answer what a modular buyer is comparing.

**What should a modular interior design business's website prioritise instead?**
Visible configuration or package options, transparent structured pricing, an explicit speed advantage where one exists, and proof of consistency across many projects rather than individual beauty shots.

**Why does blurring the position between bespoke and modular hurt conversion?**
It satisfies neither buyer fully. The site reads as neither clearly premium and custom nor clearly affordable and fast, and a blurred position converts worse than either clear one on its own.
