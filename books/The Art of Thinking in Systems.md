# The Art of Thinking in Systems — Principles

## Top 5 — Quick Reference
- Before proposing a fix, map the stocks (what accumulates), flows (in/out), and feedback loops — not just the isolated event that triggered the problem.
- Ask "what are we missing?" instead of "who's to blame?" when a problem recurs despite repeated interventions.
- Expect a delay between an intervention and its visible results — don't add more interventions just because change hasn't shown up yet.
- To change a stubborn ("policy-resistant") system, find or build a unifying goal across subsystems instead of trying to overpower the resistance.
- Change the system's rules/relationships/purpose for leverage — tweaking individual elements is usually the least effective place to intervene.

## Full Principles (10-20 points)
- Model any recurring [[_MOCs/Topics/Problem|problem]] as stocks and [[_MOCs/Topics/Flows|flows]]: identify what's accumulating (the stock), what's adding to it ([[_MOCs/Topics/Inflow|inflow]]), and what's draining it ([[_MOCs/Topics/Outflow|outflow]]) before [[_MOCs/Topics/Proposing|proposing]] a fix. #core
- Recognize dynamic equilibrium — when inflow equals outflow, the stock looks stable even though material is constantly moving through it; don't mistake apparent stability for the absence of forces acting on the system. #minor
- When a system "isn't working," first check whether it's actually performing exactly as designed for a different purpose than the one you assumed — redesign for the new purpose rather than assuming failure. #core
- Treat problems as interconnected rather than isolated — a single visible symptom (e.g., wilting plants) usually has multiple contributing causes (soil, sunlight, seed quality, season, pests) that need investigating together. #core
- Replace "who's to [[_MOCs/Topics/Blame|blame]]?" with "what are we missing?" or "what don't we understand about the problem?" as your default question when a problem persists. #core
- Expect a time delay between applying a fix and seeing results — don't pile on additional [[_MOCs/Topics/Interventions|interventions]] just because the first one hasn't shown visible impact yet; premature escalation can overshoot and destabilize the system. #core
- Look for "[[_MOCs/Topics/Policy|policy]] [[_MOCs/Topics/Resistance|resistance]]" — when repeated [[_MOCs/Topics/Fixes|fixes]] fail to move a system's behavior — as a signal that [[_MOCs/Topics/Subsystems|subsystems]] have misaligned goals pulling in different directions, not that the fixes were poorly designed. #core
- Resolve policy resistance by finding a [[_MOCs/Topics/Unifying|unifying]] goal all subsystems can genuinely agree on, rather than trying to [[_MOCs/Topics/Overpower|overpower]] resistance with more force (contrast the [[_MOCs/Topics/Romanian|Romanian]] coercive [[_MOCs/Topics/Population|population]] policy that backfired vs. Sweden's shared-goal approach that built trust). #core
- Consider "calming down" — redirecting energy toward a new approach — as an alternative to escalating a fight against resistance, since easing pressure can reduce the opposing pull as well. #minor
- When you want to change a system's behavior long-term, [[_MOCs/Topics/Intervene|intervene]] on its purpose/function and interconnections (rules, relationships) rather than on its individual [[_MOCs/Topics/Elements|elements]] — this produces much bigger leverage. #core
- Build causal loop diagrams starting small and simple, adding elements only as needed, rather than trying to map every variable at once. #minor
- Gather every stakeholder's perspective in the system before diagnosing the problem — everyone sees it through a different lens, and a full picture requires all of them. #minor
- Shift blame-focused failure analysis (e.g., "which person made the [[_MOCs/Topics/Error|error]]") toward system-focused analysis (e.g., "what about the system made this error likely") — the classic example being how the [[_MOCs/Topics/Healthcare|healthcare]] industry's move to a blame-free error-reporting culture improved patient safety more than punishing individuals did. #core
- Treat feedback from a system as a learning opportunity rather than pure output to react to — deliberately study what a result tells you about the underlying structure. #minor
- Recognize "systems archetypes" — recurring patterns of behavior that show up across many different systems over time — so you can recognize a familiar pattern faster in a new situation instead of starting from zero. #minor
- Hold your conclusions as provisional: there is no final, definitive answer in systems thinking — treat each answer as the start of a new question rather than a stopping point. #minor
- Before intervening, verify the problem is a genuine candidate for systems thinking: it's important, it's recurred over time with a traceable history, and past attempts to fix it directly have failed. #minor

## Where This Knowledge Comes From
Steven Schuster's book is a popularization/synthesis of established systems-thinking scholarship, most notably drawing on Donella Meadows's work (stocks, flows, leverage points) and citing real-world case studies (the U.S. "To Err Is Human" healthcare report, U.S. education policy history, Romanian/Hungarian/Swedish population-policy examples). It compiles and explains existing research and documented case studies rather than presenting original research of the author's own.
