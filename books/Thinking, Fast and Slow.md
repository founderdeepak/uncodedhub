# Thinking, Fast and Slow — Principles

## Top 5 — Quick Reference
- Before trusting a snap judgment, ask whether you substituted an easy question for the hard one you were actually asked.
- Distrust any strong intuitive confidence — confidence tracks the coherence of a story, not its accuracy.
- When a sample is small, expect more extreme results — don't read a pattern into noise.
- Anchor deliberately: name a number first in negotiations, and discount any number someone else names first.
- For any recurring high-stakes decision, replace gut judgment with a simple checklist or formula.

## Full Principles (10–20)
- Recognize the two-[[_MOCs/Topics/System|system]] architecture: System 1 is fast, automatic, and always running; System 2 is slow, effortful, and lazy by default — most [[_MOCs/Topics/Errors|errors]] happen because System 2 rubber-stamps System 1 instead of checking it. #core
- Watch for "What You See Is All There Is" (WYSIATI): System 1 builds a confident story from whatever evidence is in front of it, ignoring what it doesn't know it's missing — so ask explicitly "what am I not seeing?" before deciding. #core
- Diagnose substitution errors: when a question is hard (e.g., "will this [[_MOCs/Topics/Entrepreneurship|startup]] succeed?"), the mind quietly answers an easier related question (e.g., "do I like this founder?") — catch the swap by restating the original question. #core
- Use base rates before story: when given both [[_MOCs/Topics/Statistical|statistical]] base rates and a vivid individuating description (the "Tom W" / "Steve the librarian" problems), force yourself to [[_MOCs/Topics/Anchor|anchor]] on the base rate first, then adjust. #core
- Apply the law of small numbers: small samples produce more extreme and less reliable results than large ones — don't draw strong conclusions from a handful of data points (e.g., a few quarters of results, a few games). #core
- Expect regression to the mean after any unusually good or bad performance, and don't invent causal explanations (punishment worked, praise backfired) for what is really statistical reversion. #core
- Counteract anchoring by writing down your own estimate before seeing anyone else's [[_MOCs/Topics/Number|number]], and treat any first-offered number in a negotiation as a deliberate anchor to be discounted, not evidence. #core
- Use the availability heuristic check: if a [[_MOCs/Topics/Risk|risk]] feels vivid or was recently in the news, you will overweight its probability — cross-check memorable examples against actual frequency data. #core
- Build "loss aversion" into decisions: losses are felt roughly twice as intensely as equivalent gains, so expect yourself and others to be irrationally risk-averse when framed as avoiding a loss, and risk-seeking when framed as avoiding a certain loss (the fourfold pattern). #core
- Reframe decisions to escape narrow framing: evaluate a choice as one instance in a long series of similar decisions ("risk policy") rather than in isolation, which reduces overreaction to any single outcome. #core
- Watch for the endowment effect: you will demand more to give up something you own than you'd pay to acquire it — when pricing your own asset or work, deliberately ask what you'd pay as a buyer. #minor
- Use premortems before committing to a plan: imagine it has failed a year from now and write down why, to surface risks that [[_MOCs/Topics/Optimistic|optimistic]] planning bias hides. #core
- Distrust [[_MOCs/Topics/Expert|expert]] intuition in low-[[_MOCs/Topics/Validity|validity]], low-feedback environments (stock picking, long-range political [[_MOCs/Topics/Forecasting|forecasting]]); trust it in high-validity, fast-feedback environments (firefighting, chess) — check which environment you're in before deferring to a "gut expert." #core
- Prefer simple statistical [[_MOCs/Topics/Formulas|formulas]] over holistic expert [[_MOCs/Topics/Judgment|judgment]] for repeated prediction tasks (hiring, medical diagnosis, credit risk) — formulas built from a few weighted, independently-scored factors reliably outperform [[_MOCs/Topics/Intuitive|intuitive]] overall impressions. #core
- Take the outside view: before estimating your own project's timeline or cost, find the reference class of similar past projects and start from their actual outcomes, not your inside-view story. #core
- Separate the "[[_MOCs/Topics/Experiencing|experiencing]] self" from the "[[_MOCs/Topics/Remembering|remembering]] self": your memory of an experience is dominated by its peak and its ending (peak-end rule), not its duration — so if you want a good memory of an event, prioritize the finish. #minor
- Use cognitive ease deliberately: information that's easier to read, pronounce, or rhyme is believed more readily and remembered better — so make important messages simple and clear, and treat glossy/fluent presentations from others with extra skepticism. #minor
- Beware the halo effect: an initial positive impression (looks, charisma, one good result) [[_MOCs/Topics/Biases|biases]] how you interpret everything that follows about a person — order evaluations to reduce this, e.g., score independent traits separately before forming an overall opinion. #minor
- Practice "reference-class forecasting" for your own overconfidence: after making a prediction, explicitly list reasons you could be wrong before acting on it, since the planning fallacy and illusion of validity make confident forecasts systematically too optimistic. #core

## Where This Knowledge Comes From
The book distills roughly four decades of peer-reviewed experimental psychology research by Kahneman and Amos Tversky, including their landmark 1974 Science paper "Judgment Under Uncertainty: Heuristics and Biases" and later prospect theory work that won Kahneman the 2002 Nobel Prize in Economics. <cite index="1-42,1-43">That article documented systematic errors in the thinking of normal people and traced them to the design of the machinery of cognition rather than to emotion, and it went on to become one of the most highly cited works in social science. The claims are grounded in controlled lab experiments and survey studies, not anecdote, though Kahneman is explicit that some findings (especially around the "experiencing vs. remembering self") rely on smaller, more contested bodies of research than the core heuristics-and-biases work.
