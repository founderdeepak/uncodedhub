# Designing Brand Identity — Principles

## Top 5 — Quick Reference
- Start every identity project with structured research (audit, market, stakeholder interviews) before any design work.
- Define brand strategy and positioning in words before touching visuals — design should express a decided strategy, not invent one.
- Design every mark to work at the smallest size and lowest-fidelity medium first (favicon, embroidery), then scale up.
- Build a complete visual language (logo, color, type, imagery, voice) and govern it with explicit standards, not just a logo file.
- Treat brand identity as a living system that must flex across touchpoints, not a fixed static asset delivered once.

## Full Principles (10–20)
- Run the five-phase process in order — conducting research, clarifying strategy, designing identity, creating touchpoints, managing assets — and resist skipping to visual design before strategy is locked, since design decisions made without strategy tend to be arbitrary and hard to defend later. #core
- Separate "brand" from "brand identity" from "branding": brand is the gut feeling a person has about a product/company, brand identity is the tangible expression of the brand (what you can see/touch), and branding is the disciplined process of building awareness — use these as distinct diagnostic questions when a project stalls. #core
- Conduct a full brand audit (current market position, competitor landscape, internal stakeholder interviews) before proposing any creative direction, so recommendations are grounded in evidence rather than personal taste. #core
- Write a clear positioning statement and brand strategy document as the deliverable that precedes and constrains all visual design — every subsequent design decision should be traceable back to this strategy. #core
- Design the primary mark to remain legible and distinct at its smallest realistic application (favicon, app icon, embossed material) — test scalability early rather than designing only at presentation size. #core
- Build the full identity system, not just a logo: define a coordinated color palette, typography system, imagery/photography style, and voice/tone guidelines as equally load-bearing elements of the identity. #core
- Ensure the identity is distinct from competitors in its actual category — audit direct competitors' visual identities explicitly to avoid convergence, since sameness is one of the most common brand identity failures. #core
- Design for flexibility across touchpoints (digital, print, environmental, packaging, motion) from the start, rather than designing a static logo and retrofitting it to every medium afterward. #core
- Create explicit brand standards/guidelines documentation (usage rules, clear space, incorrect usage examples, color values) so the identity can be applied consistently by people who weren't in the original design process. #core
- Involve stakeholders and decision-makers early and often in the research and strategy phases, not just at final presentation — buy-in secured early reduces costly late-stage rework and rejection. #minor
- Prototype the identity across a representative range of real touchpoints (business card, website, signage, packaging) before finalizing, since a mark that looks strong in isolation can fail in context. #minor
- Name the brand elements deliberately (naming, tagline, verbal identity) as part of the same coordinated system as the visual identity, since verbal and visual identity should reinforce a single strategic idea. #minor
- Plan a phased rollout and asset management system for launch, since even a strong identity fails in the market if the transition/rollout and internal training are poorly managed. #minor
- Revisit and evolve identity systems periodically rather than treating "before and after" redesigns as a rare, one-time event — brand identity should be actively managed over the brand's life, not shelved after launch. #minor

## Where This Knowledge Comes From
Alina Wheeler draws on her own decades of experience as a brand identity consultant, structured around a proven five-phase process framework, illustrated throughout with named real-world case studies (client engagements referenced in the book's Best Practices/case study section) rather than academic research. This is a professional practitioner's methodology text — its authority rests on applied design-consulting experience and observed industry practice, not controlled studies.
